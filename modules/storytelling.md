# Storytelling for Interactive Installations

## At a crossroads

Interactive installations sit at the meeting point of three fields:

| Field | What it brings | What it lacks |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Film** | Immersion, pacing, visual language, strong narrative arcs | Interactivity |
| **Games** | Interaction, rules, mechanics, player agency | Bodily immersion |
| **Experiences**  | Physical space, social presence, the body | Usually less narrative depth |

Our work is often *story-driven*, and we can borrow freely from all three. A film may not be interactive, but it still tells a story, and the way it builds that story is useful to us. The same goes for the way a theme-park ride or an exhibition guides people through a narrative.

This also means there are, in practice, a thousand and one techniques you could use to come up with a story. This chapter does not try to cover them all. It gives you a practical toolkit, a structure, a world-building method, and a way to turn the result into an installation.

**Warm-up**. Think of the last story you experienced: a series, a film, a game, a book. What is it about in one sentence? Keep it in mind; we will use it in the next section.

## Where do stories come from? Starting techniques

### What if…?

The *what-if* is the core construct behind a huge number of stories. You take our world and change **one fundamental aspect** of it. Only one. You can change more, and some stories do, but a good what-if world can be traced back to a single change.

- *Lucifer*: what if the devil were real, and came to live on earth?  
- *Toy Story*: what if toys could talk and had feelings? Pixar's catalogue is essentially one long series of what-ifs.  
- Marvel's *What If…?* series takes a known story and changes one event each episode. That is a double what-if, because the Avengers themselves already rest on what-ifs: what if the Norse gods were real? What if aliens existed?

**Rule of thumb:** if you can't name the one thing that is different about your world, your what-if isn't clear yet.

### Real events

Here you don't change the world; you start from things that really happened (or are claimed to have happened) and build from there. You may exaggerate, dramatise or fictionalise, but the core is real.

- *The Conjuring* is based on a real couple, Ed and Lorraine Warren, who investigated hauntings.  
- *Annabelle*, from the same series, is based on a doll that really exists. In the film it is made far creepier than the real thing.

"Inspired by true events" is a category in its own right, and a powerful one: the audience's knowledge that "this could happen" does part of the work for you.

### Mechanic-first

Especially common in games: you start from a **mechanic** (an interaction or technical effect) and only afterwards ask what story could give it meaning.

- *Rain World* began as a technical demo of procedural animation (inverse kinematics) that made a creature's limbs move realistically. An ecosystem was built around it, and only then came the story: a small, fragile creature that has lost its family and must survive.  
- In *Doom*, the core mechanic of the first-person shooter is *you aim by looking*. Everything else follows from that.

**Installation example.** "Wouldn't it be cool if a trail appeared behind you as you walked through the space?" Nice, but what story goes with it? Perhaps: the land is barren, and you are the one whose footsteps make flowers grow. Why? Now you are building a story. You are **creating meaning to serve your original mechanic**.

### Modal shift

You take an existing concept and give it a different **medium or modality**.

- Top-down shooters (think of the very first *GTA*) became third-person 3D games.  
- *Factorio* (2D factory building) in 3D is, roughly, *Satisfactory*.  
- Theme-park attractions turn existing films into rides.

Ask yourself: *what would this story look like as an object, as an installation, as a VR experience?* Take *Little Red Riding Hood* as VR and the questions pile up immediately. Are you Little Red Riding Hood in first person? Are you the wolf?

### Persona switch

Retell a known story from **someone else's perspective**.

- *Loki* is a whole series built on this idea. Marvel does it often: what does this look like from Spider-Man's point of view? From the villain's?  
- You can apply it to your own life. Suppose you were once almost in a car accident. Tell the story from the point of view of the other driver. What did it do to *them*?

This is one of the most common story-writing techniques, and it works on almost anything.

### Taking figures of speech literally (and other tricks)

Some stories and games take a figurative expression literally. The Dutch saying *de lange arm hebben* ("to have a long arm", meaning to have far-reaching influence) has a counterpart in games where a character has endlessly stretching arms, used to solve puzzles such as picking someone's pocket for a key to open a door.

Playing with **perspective** is related. A small object held close to the camera can look like a giant sun. That is more a mechanic than a story, but it is exactly the kind of starting point from which a story can grow.

## The building blocks of a story

Almost every story contains the same set of recurring elements. The best-known model is Joseph Campbell's **hero's journey**, popularised for writers by Christopher Vogler. *Star Wars: Episode IV* is the textbook example: Luke begins on a farm, needs a reason to leave his comfort zone, sets out on an adventure, hits a low point when Obi-Wan dies, is trained, and finally overcomes his crisis.

This chapter splits the journey into slightly different pieces, for a reason that will become clear in Section 6\.

### The elements

**Two worlds**

- **Common world:** where the story always starts, the hero's everyday life.  
- **Special world:** where everything else happens.  
- The hero ends back in the common world, but **upgraded**: changed, with something new.

**Hero** The main character. This can be an **antihero**, a hero who is not very heroic.

**Antagonist** The opposing force. It does not have to be a person: it can be a system, nature, time or the hero's own fear.

**Wisdom (guidance)** Someone or something that helps: Gandalf in *The Lord of the Rings*, Obi-Wan in *Star Wars*. In many games this is the tutorial, the general or the guide who explains how things work.

**Event (call to action)** Something happens that gives the hero a **goal**. Without it the hero would stay content in their world, with no reason to go anywhere. The event can be huge (a comet almost hits the earth) or very small (the death of a family member, which leaves you wanting to find out what happened).

**Goal** What the hero wants to achieve because of the event.

**Departure** The step from the common world into the special world.

**Crisis** The low point or climax. Somewhere in the arc the hero must reach a moment where it seems impossible: *how on earth are we going to solve this?* In games this is often the **final boss**: you die a few times before you get through.

**Resolution (resurrection)** The hero overcomes the crisis.

**Elixir** What the hero brings back to the common world. This is a blanket term: it can be a magical object that fixes the problem, or simply the fact that the problem is gone (the Ring has been destroyed, so all is well again). It can also be inner change: Luke overcoming his fear.

**Key** Sometimes an extra element is added: the piece of wisdom, the object or the skill you need to get through the crisis. This is often more a **game mechanic** than a story element. Perseverance alone is not enough; you have to find the key first. That makes it a story-*driving* element, because the hero must go after it before the story can continue.

A story can run through several of these cycles, but this is the core.

### You can start anywhere

Each of these elements can be your starting point. Choose one, define it, and the others start to follow.

**Starting from the hero: Marcus**

> ##### `We're telling a story about Marcus. Marcus is a 27-year-old… robot.`

From that one line:

- **World:** probably futuristic (or steampunk, but let's lock it: futuristic).  
- **Crisis:** what is the worst thing a robot can experience? Its power source failing. Near-death, or death itself.  
- **Elixir:** consciousness, self-determination, eternal power, or a world where robots are seen as equals.  
- If we choose *equality*, that defines the **goal**, and the **event** follows from it: Marcus's best friend, another robot, is scrapped, because robots have no right to self-determination.  
- That in turn tells us more about the **world**: a class system in which robots are clearly inferior. At 27, Marcus is probably a bit outdated himself.

From "Marcus is a 27-year-old robot" you could build a whole game, film or installation. Perhaps you see the world through his eyes the whole time. Perhaps, as an installation, Marcus stands in the middle of the room and you have to find components for him.

**Starting from an event: dinosaurs from the canal**

> ##### `Overnight, the canal next to the school splits open and dinosaurs climb out.`

That is the event and the call to action. Who is the hero? If it happens here, probably a student. A multimedia student. The goal? *Save the school.* And we're off.

Many installations start from real-world events: a flooded home, or the story of fleeing your home and migrating.

**Starting from the world: 1989, but…**

Starting from the world is one of the most common approaches. Look at how *Dungeons & Dragons* works: there is a created world, and although some game masters work with a story structure, most of the time goes into the world itself. If you start from a rich world and build on it, things tend to fall into place.

> ##### `The year is 1989. What's special about this world? Pirates are still absolutely real and a dominant power. (Other ideas from the workshop: we never learned to control the virus; the dot-com bubble ended in something AI-like; dinosaurs still roam; magic is real, but only if you pay enough for it.)`

Now ask who lives here. A pirate, or someone who isn't? If you are not a pirate, the event might be that pirates rob you, and your goal is revenge. If you *are* a pirate: why? You were sold to pirates as a child. Your goal is to find your family. The call to action: you find a letter, or a medallion, or you fall overboard, wash up on an island and someone says, "Hey, aren't you the Johnson kid?" And you set off.

## Building a world: the GRAPES framework

There are heavier frameworks for world-building. One of the most widely used is **GRAPES**, which describes a world through six aspects:

| Letter | Aspect | Key questions |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **G** | **Geography** | What is the physical world? Where and when is it? Space, under water, here, virtual? |
| **R** | **Religion** | What do people believe? One religion, several, a cult, a belief system that holds the whole world together? Is religion tied to power? |
| **A** | **Achievements** | What has this civilisation achieved? Art, architecture, technology? Did it once get far and then fall back? |
| **P** | **Politics** | Who makes the rules? Kings, presidents, anarchy? How are the rules enforced? |
| **E** | **Economics** | How is money made? Is there money, or barter? What is most valuable? |
| **S** | **Society** | How is society structured? Who matters, who doesn't? Is there a class system? |

Remember that Campbell gives you *two* worlds. If your story really has a split between a common and a special world, you can define both separately with GRAPES.

### GRAPES in practice

Take our pirate world and run it through each letter:

- **Geography:** at sea, among many small islands. Or: forget it, *space pirates*, raiding between planets, with the goal of breaking up the colonies.  
- **Religion:** perhaps people believe in the Singularity. It hasn't come yet, but everyone believes it will and that it will fix everything.  
- **Achievements:** everyone has a fancy ship with no power problems. Or: technologically we never got much further than the Saturn V rocket. You still strap someone into a rocket and hope they bring back the right loot. Suddenly that's a completely different world.  
- **Politics:** kings, presidents, or pure anarchy?  
- **Economics:** is there money, or only barter?  
- **Society:** who is important on board, and who isn't?

### Examples from film and series

- **Dune**: *religion and economics.* For the people of the desert planet, the belief system revolves around moisture and water, with traditions and rituals tied to it. For others, it includes the rule that you don't use thinking computers. The most valuable thing is the spice.  
- **In Time**: *economics and society.* The currency is time. Otherwise the world is not very different from ours. Because time is money, society splits into people who can live forever and people who live day to day.  
- **Arcane**: *society.* Geographically there is only one world, but two classes live in it: those down below and those up above. Describe the two against each other and friction appears automatically.

### How the aspects connect

The six aspects can be linked or deliberately kept separate, and both choices create story.

- **Linked:** the more money you have, the higher your status.  
- **Separate:** there is a fixed class system. You will never move up, however rich you are. So there are lower-class people with enormous wealth who are still looked down on, and very poor people of high class.  
- **Religion \+ politics \+ economics:** a sect is in power, decides by "divine intervention" what is allowed, and controls the money.

You can even invert a whole aspect. Geography doesn't have to be physical: suppose everyone lives in a virtual world, and because electronics have broken down nobody can get out. There is one last working virtual device that might be the way out. Now your geography is purely virtual, and the question becomes: what does that world look like?

Another example: *gravity is controllable*. Building a house becomes completely different. You can pay someone to make your house float, with a five-year guarantee, and hang a glass greenhouse tunnel underneath it, because why not. What does controllable gravity mean for religion? Is it a gift of the gods? For society? Perhaps only a few can control gravity, they are the "gods" of the system, and certain cities float out of everyone else's reach.

> **Tip.** You don't need to define everything in depth. Define a few things, choose what serves your story, and prune later.

## How much story fits? Depth versus time

You can go through the full cycle very quickly, and you can even skip a few steps, as long as you think about how your world works and what happens in it.

But there is a hard rule:

> **The less time you have, the less far and the less extreme you can take your world.**

Suppose a ten-year-old has to get through your installation in a few minutes. You can talk about pirates or space. But by the time you've explained that only Saturn V rockets exist and nothing else, because of this and that, you've lost them.

You *can* say: "Bears walk upright, do ordinary jobs, and live among us." That's instantly clear. You *can't* explain that this is due to an economic shift that once happened in Soviet Russia. That goes much too far.

A book gives you hours to unfold a world calmly. An installation does not. Everything that doesn't directly serve the visitor's short experience gets cut, however good it is.

## From story to installation

### The visitor is the hero

For the installations in this course, one decision is made for you: **The hero is the visitor.**

You could make other choices. The visitor could be the antagonist, or a bystander who watches the story without directly influencing it (or who has a god-like influence, with everything changing to make the hero act). Those are all valid options. But here we start from the visitor as hero, so you don't need to invent one.

The target group in this workshop is **children between five and ten years old**, and the experience should not take longer than about **ten minutes**. The aim is a **single-interaction installation**: one clear action that resolves the story.

### Mapping the journey onto an installation

The story elements become design questions:

| Story element | In an installation it means… | Example |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Call to adventure** | What draws the visitor in? What brings them to your installation? | In an underwater world, you have to step through a submarine door to get in. That door is your call to adventure: *come on, let's play.* |
| **Wisdom** | What does the visitor need to know, and how do you tell them? How do you explain the rules of your world? | Screens, sound, objects, or (the flattest option) a sign with the rules. |
| **Key** | The thing the visitor needs to take the next step or solve the story. | Often the object or knowledge that makes the action possible. |
| **Crisis** | What pushes the visitor to actually act? | Things get unfair, urgent or absurd until doing nothing is no longer an option. |
| **Resolution** | What does the visitor's action do? How does it fix things? | The world responds and changes. |
| **Elixir** | What does the visitor take home? The final takeaway. | An insight, a feeling, a small piece of empowerment. |

The call to action says *come into my story*. The crisis has to push: *and now we're going to change something*.

The crisis must make the visitor *want* to act. The child does not have to almost die (please don't), but they should get the urge: *I have to do something here.* In a world where gravity can be switched on and off, maybe there is a big mechanical switch they must flip at the right moment, or a small surfboard they must step onto to ride a gravity wave somewhere.

**Don't think about technology yet.** How a voice is captured, how images adapt, how it's wired: that comes later. First make sure the story works.

### Worked example: the megaphone on the mountain

This is the lecturer's own concept, developed during the workshop from three random cards.

**The cards:** *a beautiful megaphone* \+ *in a world where kings never stopped ruling*.

**The world (GRAPES, briefly):**

- **Geography:** a mountaintop, high in the mountains.  
- **Religion:** people believe the king is chosen by the mountain.  
- **Achievements / art:** a beautiful megaphone requires smithing, fine jewellery, craftsmanship.  
- **Politics:** the king decides everything. The royal guard enforces it.  
- **Economics:** the currency is *seconds with the king*. If I buy a loaf of bread from you, it costs me two seconds of the king's attention. If the king decides everything, having his ear is the most valuable thing there is.  
- **Society:** those at court sell access to the king. They trade in the king's attention.

**The story, mapped onto the installation:**

- **Call to adventure:** a large, beautiful megaphone stands in the middle of the space and invites you to approach.  
- **Wisdom:** all around, screens show the king talking to people and making decrees. "From now on, everyone wears only red," and all the screens turn red. The visitor learns the rules of the world: *what the king says, happens.* (The flattest version would be a sign: "When the king speaks, you are silent. When the king has decided, it is decided." That is also wisdom, just the most boring way to deliver it. Once you know your rules, ask how you can show them visually or through sound instead.)  
- **Crisis:** the decrees become more and more unfair the longer you watch. "The king has decided that nobody with yellow shoes may walk in town." "The king has decided that ten-year-olds may no longer play outside." The child is pushed towards action.  
- **Resolution:** the child shouts through the megaphone, and the world changes. Suddenly the child is in charge. What would they shout? Perhaps a refusal. In a world where the king's word is law, *refusing* might be the greatest power there is. Perhaps other voices join in and the crowd follows.  
- **Elixir:** children can speak up. They have a voice in this world.

**What was cut:** an earlier idea was that the megaphone's resonance would make the mountain tremble and threaten the king's power. That would need a completely different story, with the "chosen by the mountain" rule as the central wisdom. The court selling the king's attention was also cut. It is a great mechanic for a book, where there are hours to explain it, but it doesn't serve a ten-minute installation for children. Keep it for another project.

even with a rich world in your head, the installation uses only what the five points need. Draw the visitor in, give them the right knowledge, push them to act, let the action matter, send them home with something.

## Workshop: the card method

This exercise gets you from nothing to a pitchable installation concept in a few hours. Instead of building a whole world from scratch, it uses shortcuts.

### Step 1 — Fill three stacks of cards

Write **one idea per card**. Don't think too long. Classic tropes are welcome; think of films too.

1. **Worlds (what-ifs).** "A world that is completely under water." "A world where animals can talk." "A world where everyone has a double." "A world where everyone is blind." "A world where gravity is controllable." "A world where being a pirate is just a normal job." "A world without hierarchy." "A world without technology."  
2. **Objects.** Ordinary, everyday objects, large or small: a fork, a door, a camera, a car, a surfboard, a magnifying glass.  
3. **Modifiers.** Adjectives: beautiful, broken, giant, small, old, wet, imaginary.

### Step 2 — Draw and combine

Take **three cards from each stack, at random** (not the top ones, and don't choose). Combine them into lines of the form:

> ##### `[modifier] [object] in a world where [what-if]`

Examples from the workshop:

- a beautiful megaphone in a world where kings never stopped ruling  
- a broken door in a flooded world  
- a broken door in a world where everyone is blind  
- a small surfboard in a world where gravity is controllable  
- a giant magnifying glass in a world without hierarchy  
- an imaginary metro

You may swap **one** card between lines if you like. You don't have to.

### Step 3 — Choose one

Pick the combination you want to work with. That's your story world.

### Step 4 — Define the world with GRAPES

For each of the six aspects, write a short definition of what it looks like in your world. Don't hold too tightly to a scenario yet; there are still many possibilities. While you do this you will probably start to see a story. Let it in, but keep it simple: chains of reasoning like "if they get X, then they can do Y, and then Z" are already too far for a single-interaction installation.

### Step 5 — Map it to the five points

Call to adventure → wisdom (and key) → crisis → resolution → elixir. Remember: the visitor is the hero, the visitor is a child of five to ten, and they have about ten minutes.

### Step 6 — Make it visual

The more visual, the better. That doesn't mean finished; it means sketchy. A floor plan, a quick drawing: "you come in from here, this is what you see." Under water? Maybe there's a periscope you have to look through.

**Wizard of Oz prototyping** works very well here. Set it up and play the installation yourself: "I am the computer. You come in, you see this, you hear this." Someone says, "I shout into the microphone that it's not fair." "OK, you do that, and *this* happens." That gives everyone a clear picture straight away.

### Step 7 — Pitch

Explain briefly how your installation fits together: your story, your rules, and the five points. It's fine if you haven't worked everything out yet.

## Story and interaction: affordances, signifiers and feedback

In earlier sessions we looked at interaction at its first level using the **Interaction Frogger framework** (Wensveen, Djajadiningrat & Overbeeke), which works with three big concepts:

- **Affordances:** what an object communicates about its purpose purely through its *form*. (We will come back to this later.)  
- **Feedback:** you do something, and the object responds. A megaphone needs to give feedback when I speak into it. Perhaps a small light switches on, so it's clear the object reacts to my presence.  
- **Signifiers:** things you *add* to an object to give it meaning: labels, icons, and so on.

**Signifiers are where storytelling and interaction meet.** Learning to tell a story is not a separate, dead skill: an installation always gains when there is a story behind it, and the story is a route into signifiers. Any megaphone will do the job technically, but the signifiers you add make clear *why* it is there and *what* it does.

Two versions of the same megaphone tell two different stories:

- **A relic from the past.** It stands in a museum-like display case with a little door you have to open first.  
- **An object on the public square** that nobody dares to use, because… you don't speak over the king, do you?

Same object, completely different meaning, and each choice tells your story differently.

## 

## Summary

- Installations sit between film, games and experiences, and can borrow storytelling from all three.  
- Six ways to start a story: **what-if** (change one fundamental thing), **real events**, **mechanic-first** (create meaning for a mechanic), **modal shift** (new medium for an existing concept), **persona switch** (another point of view), and **taking expressions literally**.  
- The hero's journey provides the building blocks: common/special world, hero, antagonist, wisdom, event, goal, departure, crisis, resolution, elixir and key. **You can start from any of them.**  
- **GRAPES** (Geography, Religion, Achievements, Politics, Economics, Society) gives you a quick, structured way to build a world. Tension comes from how the aspects connect, or deliberately don't.  
- **The time you have limits the story you can tell.** In a short installation, cut everything that doesn't serve the visitor's experience.  
- In an installation **the visitor is the hero**: call to adventure draws them in, wisdom explains the rules, the crisis pushes them to act, the resolution makes their action matter, and the elixir is what they take home.  
- Story feeds interaction design through **signifiers**: the meaning you add to an object.

## Key terms

| Term | Definition |
| :---- | :---- |
| **What-if** | A story premise that changes one fundamental aspect of our world. |
| **Mechanic-first** | Designing an interaction or effect first, then creating a story that gives it meaning. |
| **Modal shift** | Translating an existing concept into another medium or modality. |
| **Persona switch** | Retelling a story from another character's perspective. |
| **Common world / special world** | The hero's everyday world and the world where the adventure happens. |
| **Call to action / event** | What gives the hero a reason to leave the common world; in an installation, what draws the visitor in. |
| **Wisdom** | Guidance given to the hero; in an installation, how the rules of the world are communicated. |
| **Key** | The object or knowledge needed to get through the crisis; often a game mechanic. |
| **Crisis** | The low point or climax; in an installation, the push that makes the visitor act. |
| **Elixir** | What the hero brings back; in an installation, the final takeaway. |
| **GRAPES** | World-building framework: Geography, Religion, Achievements, Politics, Economics, Society. |
| **Wizard of Oz prototyping** | Simulating an interactive system by having a person play its role. |
| **Affordance** | What an object's form communicates about how to use it. |
| **Signifier** | An added cue (label, icon, context) that gives an object meaning. |
| **Feedback** | The response an object gives after an action. |
