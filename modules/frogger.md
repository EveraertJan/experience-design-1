# The Frogger Framework, Feedback and Feedforward

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you can:

- explain what an interaction is and why the definition is deliberately broad;  
- dissect an existing interaction using the six aspects **time, location, direction, modality, expression and dynamics**;  
- explain the difference between **feedback** and **feedforward**, and between **functional, augmented and inherent** information;  
- explain why digital interactions feel less intuitive than physical ones, and what a designer can do about it;  
- deliberately make an existing interaction more playful by playing with those six aspects.

## What is an interaction?

We start from a deliberately broad definition:

> *An interaction is a mutual or reciprocal action, influence or communication between two or more people, objects or forces, where each party affects or is affected by the other.*

Three things stand out.

**Reciprocity.** Both parties (the *actors*) are engaged with each other. They influence each other, communicate with each other, or impose something on each other.

**Actors are not only people.** They can also be objects or forces. You and a door: an interaction. One object and another object: an interaction. Wind blowing against a house: also an interaction.

**Both parties change.** Someone who grabs a door handle changes the door (it opens), but is also affected. You feel the cold of the metal, you move your arm, and you gain a new way to enter the space.

### The grey zone

With this definition the boundary becomes blurry. When you watch a theatre play, are you interacting? The play affects you (you get emotions), but your presence doesn't change the play. That is not reciprocal, so it is probably not an interaction. As soon as the audience as a whole does influence the play, that shifts.

Interaction is therefore not an on/off switch. It is a sliding scale.

### Scale and chains

You can look at interactions at different levels:

- **between people**, or between people and objects;  
- **in chains:** you type on your PC, your PC talks to the network, the network to a server, that server to someone else's PC, which in turn talks to that person. Did you interact with that person? In a sense yes, but via a long detour and with media in between;  
- **societally:** an interactive experience about migration can change your view of migration, and your view in turn contributes to how society thinks about it.

You can make an interaction as large or as small as you like. In this chapter we zoom in on one small, concrete interaction at a time.

## The Frogger framework

The framework is called **Frogger** and comes from a design paper (Wensveen, Djajadiningrat & Overbeeke, 2004). It looks at how **action** and **function** are coupled through **feedback** and **feedforward**. The paper is on Canvas.

The starting point is a question: what is the coupling between what you do and the information you get back from the object you are interacting with?

Take the door you try to pull when you should have pushed. The door *told* you to pull. That was wrong information. Your action was based on that information, so something went wrong.

### Guidelines for free and playful interactions

The paper gives a number of guidelines for interactions in which people can experiment freely:

1. **Make actions easily reversible.** This is the most important one. If someone is afraid that an action can't be undone, they will think twice, and that is exactly what you don't want in a playful interaction. With a magic mirror, the user steps out of frame and everything resets to zero. Nothing is broken, nothing went wrong, and you can start again.  
2. **Make full use of the user's perceptual-motor skills.** That means seeing, feeling, hearing and smelling, but also moving and grasping. Involve as many **modalities** as possible.  
3. **Offer multiple routes to the same functionality.** If there is only one path from A to B ("stand on the dot and something happens"), there is no room to play. A mirror that responds to your movement is multimodal: you can stand still, move quickly back and forth, come closer or move away, and each time you get a different response.  
4. **Let the user act at multiple points at once.** Use sound or light in addition to a camera for poses, for example. Or use the camera not only for poses but also to detect where someone stands and how many people are in the frame.  
5. **Give information in small pieces, at the moment it is needed.** No manual at the door, because that immediately limits what is possible. Let the system guide the user step by step.

**Note: sometimes limiting is exactly the point.** For an installation in Antwerp, fish were projected on the floor. People standing on the floor saw the fish swim towards them or away. The installation shouldn't invite people to stay long, because they would then stand in the way. A playful interaction must therefore also fit its context. Someone building a fake checkpoint at an airport limits the interactions on purpose, because that is how it works in the real world too.

### The six aspects of action and function

Frogger dissects every interaction using six aspects. Each time the question is how **your action** and **the product's reaction** relate to each other.

| Aspect | Question you ask | Example of a mismatch |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Time** | How much time is there between action and reaction? Do they happen together, or delayed? | You badge and it takes three seconds before the door opens. Meanwhile you badge again. |
| **Location** | Do action and reaction happen in the same place? | A remote control: you press here, the TV reacts over there. |
| **Direction** | Does the reaction move in the same direction as the action? | A door handle you push down while the lock moves the other way. |
| **Modality** | Do the sensory channels of action and reaction match (seeing, feeling, hearing, making)? | You press a button (touch) and expect an image, but the TV stays black. |
| **Expression** | Can the user express themselves in the action, and does the reaction reflect that? | A click is always the same click, however angry or careful you make it. |
| **Dynamics** | Do the force, speed and acceleration of action and reaction correspond? | You badge calmly and the gates fly open aggressively. |

A few nuances:

- **Time versus dynamics.** Time is about the *duration* between action and reaction (instant or delayed). Dynamics is about the *intensity and speed* of it. A small knock leading to a big knock is a dynamics difference. How quickly the reaction starts is a matter of time.  
- **Location can be vague.** With a badge and a gate there is half a metre between them, but you bridge that while walking. Your own location changes too. Draw such situations on paper.  
- **Modality is about harmony, not sameness.** The modalities don't have to be identical, but they should fit together logically. You press a remote (touch) and expect to see something. On projectors, a blinking status light or a beep doesn't restore that harmony, but it does provide information while the lamp warms up.  
- **Expression is close to playfulness.** You can open a door gently or angrily. You can push a chair back angrily. You can't click angrily on a button on a touchscreen. What would "angry clicking" look like in an email program? That is where playing begins.  
- **Acceleration** is how quickly you reach a certain speed.

### Describe first, solve later

While dissecting, you don't think of **solutions** yet. You only describe and classify. For each aspect you ask what happens, not what could be better.

It helps to do this **on paper**. Draw the action, the reaction, the distance between them and the directions of movement. That is faster and clearer than writing it out.

## Unification and non-unification

### In the physical world everything coincides

Take a punching bag.

- **Time:** you hit and you feel and hear it immediately.  
- **Location:** where you hit is where it happens.  
- **Direction:** the bag moves along in the direction of your punch.  
- **Dynamics:** the harder you hit, the further the bag moves and the louder it sounds.  
- **Expression:** you can express your anger by hitting differently or harder.  
- **Modality:** you hit with your hand and feel it in your hand.

In the physical world these six aspects are almost always **aligned**. The paper calls this **unification**. It gives a natural coupling between action and consequence.

### Digital and electronic pull that coupling apart

As soon as an interaction becomes electronic, the aspects can drift apart. A light switch here can turn on a lamp over there. That is convenient (you don't have to walk to the lamp), but it costs something: you now have to **know** that this switch belongs to that lamp.

This **non-unification** gives new functionality, but also demands **new information** to guide the user. The further you pull the aspects apart, the harder it becomes to make the interaction user-friendly.

**Example: heating.** If facility management turns off the heating of a building, location is already no longer aligned. Is the switch an on/off button or a dial for the temperature? Time also drifts apart: the radiator is off immediately, but the room only cools down after a while. What do you show? "It's off", or the falling temperature?

You can solve this by adding extra information: a click, a light, the set temperature next to the current temperature. A more creative solution is a knob that is as warm as the room. Turn it lower and it gets colder, while a bar shows how hard the system is working.

### Interfaces

An interface is often seen as "a screen with buttons", but it is broader. Every object has an interface: its shape and layout tell you what you can do with it. An interface tries to bring back the intuitiveness lost through non-unification. But interfaces also often stand in the way of an intuitive interaction.

A laptop is a good example. The screen closes with a click and feels slightly magnetic. That is feedback, and at the same time feedforward for next time: you know you need some force to open it. Sending a chat message is actually very unnatural: you talk to someone with a delay in time, modality, location and direction. We grew up with it, but for our grandparents it isn't intuitive.

### Is intuitive always better?

Usually yes, because something you can "read" and play with is more interesting than something you can't get a grip on. But **non-intuitive can be a deliberate choice**. If you make an experience about how hard it is to go through an immigration procedure in Belgium in a language you don't master, you want it to feel non-intuitive. That is the point of the experience.

## Feedback and feedforward

### The difference

- **Feedforward** is information you get **before or during** your action, before it is finished. It tells you what you can do and how.  
- **Feedback** is information you get **after** your action is finished. It tells you what has happened.

Example: you press a button.

- The shape of the button and the slight resistance under your finger as you press it are feedforward.  
- The click, the light and the motor starting to run are feedback.

The two can play with each other. Suppose you step in front of a screen and your silhouette appears as flowers. That is feedback for you. If you walk away and a trace of those flowers lingers for a while, that is feedforward for the next visitor. They see what is possible.

### A door as an example

Opening a door is a large interaction.

- **Feedforward:** the door is ajar, you see a handle, or there is an emergency exit sign.  
- **Feedback:** the door is open and you can look through it.

You can also zoom in on **micro-interactions**. For the door handle, the shape counts as feedforward: it shows which hand you should use (nobody grabs a handle with the wrong hand). Pushing it down is an interaction of its own. That you then had to pull instead of push is a second interaction in which the feedforward was wrong. The two share an object, but are separate interactions within the larger whole.

### Limits of the classification

The classification is not entirely rigid. With long or repeated interactions (you turn the same lever several times), it isn't always clear when feedforward turns into feedback. The model is a thinking aid: *what information did I get beforehand, and what afterwards?*

### Three types of information

Both feedback and feedforward can be of three types.

**Functional** Information the system itself generates while performing its function. You press the button and the motor starts running: the function has been accomplished. That the button clicks or the motor makes a sound also falls under this. As feedforward: you see a button and know you can switch something on with it.

**Augmented** Extra information the maker adds **on top**. A light indicating that the motor is running, an icon, a text, a signal. This layer often appeals to your **cognitive skills**: you have to read or interpret it. A green light is fairly unambiguous, but with an orange light you start asking questions.

**Inherent** The natural consequences of the material and the shape. The counter-pressure of a button on your finger is always there; you can't "design" it or leave it out. You can work with it, though. As feedforward: a door handle that makes clear to your hand how to grip it, or a recess that clearly invites your fingers. You also never put a handle on a sliding door, because that information wouldn't be right.

### The complete model

The paper's model shows how everything fits together:

- At the **lowest level**, every object has functional components, augmented components and action possibilities (what you can do with it).  
- From these comes information in six forms: **{inherent, augmented, functional} × {feedforward, feedback}**.  
- An action passes through the layers each time: from inherent to augmented to functional, and back.  
- On each of those six planes you can apply the six aspects (time, location, direction, modality, expression, dynamics).

That produces many points to work on. The goal is not to draw one button press out completely, but to see where you can **play**.

## Designing playfully: the method

This is the core of the chapter. You have mapped out an interaction. Now you look at what you can do with it.

### Step 1: choose one interaction

Choose a small, well-defined interaction. Three filters help with choosing:

1. **It contains an electrical or electronic component.** Purely physical interaction is not enough.  
2. **It is a single action-reaction.** Not too complex and not too vague. "I press the button and nothing happens" is fine. "I badge at the metro and the gates fly open aggressively" too. "If I do x and then y, then that happens" is too much.  
3. **It takes place in the physical world.**

### Step 2: map it out on paper

Draw all the components and describe each of the six aspects. Don't come up with solutions yet.

### Step 3: add the information layers

What feedforward and feedback is there now? Which is functional, augmented or inherent?

### Step 4: play with the aspects

Now change each aspect, purely hypothetically. The question is not yet "can I build this?", but "what is possible?".

## Worked examples

### The badge and the gate

A badge scans an RFID tag, and a motor opens the gate. You could couple those directly. But what if you play with the aspects?

- **Time:** the gate only opens after five seconds of standing still. Or you badge for the next person in the queue. Or someone else has to stand there first before the gate opens.  
- **Location:** you don't badge on the gate itself, but somewhere else.  
- **Direction and gesture:** you have to badge in a particular way, otherwise the gate stays shut.  
- **Expression:** you can scan angrily, carefully or curiously. The gates then open angrily, carefully or hesitantly.  
- **Feedforward:** the badge reader looks like a patch of grass, or a flat pad you have to rub over briefly. You will then badge differently without thinking about it.

### The elevator button

An elevator button has a time delay and a location difference (you stand here, the elevator is there). The direction doesn't match: you press a button, while the elevator goes up or down. A slide switch would be more logical in that respect. Once pressed, you also can't undo it. How playful can you make the button?

### The light switch in the dark

You enter a dark room. You know the switch is next to the door, but not at what height. Here you can mainly play with feedforward. What if the lamp knows you are coming in and first "bows" towards you before the light comes on?

### The doorbell

You press at the door and you should hear something inside. The location therefore differs, and that makes it frustrating: you don't know whether it went off. There is expression, though: you can press hard or soft, short or long.

## Summary

- An **interaction** is reciprocal: both parties (people, objects or forces) influence each other. The boundary is blurry.  
- The **Frogger framework** couples action and function via feedback and feedforward.  
- The **six aspects**: time, location, direction, modality, expression and dynamics. In the physical world they coincide (**unification**). Electronics pulls them apart (**non-unification**), which requires extra information.  
- **Feedforward** comes before or during the action, **feedback** after it.  
- Information is **functional**, **augmented** (added, often cognitive) or **inherent** (follows from shape and material).  
- For **playful interactions**: make actions reversible, involve many modalities, offer multiple routes, let users act at several points at once, and give information step by step.  
- First you **describe** the interaction, then you **play** with it.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| :---- | :---- |
| Interaction | Reciprocal action or influence between people, objects or forces |
| Actor | A party in an interaction |
| Unification | The six aspects of action and reaction coincide |
| Non-unification | The aspects drift apart, which requires new information |
| Feedforward | Information before or during the action |
| Feedback | Information after the action is finished |
| Functional information | What the system does while performing its function |
| Augmented information | Extra information added by the maker (often cognitive) |
| Inherent information | Information that follows from shape and material |
| Modality | A sensory channel (seeing, feeling, hearing, making) |
| Interface | Everything that communicates an object's functionality through shape and layout |
